Listening for
water leaks.

Using spatial pressure changes to detect leaks in simulated urban water networks.

Context
Master’s thesis · Cal State LA
My contribution
Pressure features, ML experiments & simulation pipeline
Status
Simulation study · Physical validation is future work

The research question

Can a pattern of pressure changes across a water network distinguish leak conditions from normal operation? My thesis studied this using LeakDB benchmark data and an EPANET-based simulation pipeline.

Detection was the evaluated task. Identifying affected zones and localizing leak nodes were broader research objectives; the presentation does not report separate localization results.

From pressure readings to predictions.

  1. SimulateUse EPANET / WNTR to model hydraulic behavior and generate controlled network data.
  2. RepresentExpress each observation as 22 pressure residual features across the network.
  3. CompareEvaluate Random Forest alongside SVM and XGBoost classifiers.
  4. EvaluateExamine accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and a confusion matrix.

Methodology documented in thesis presentation, slides 6–12 and 17.

The key idea: pressure residuals.

21,120 simulated samples

The study used LeakDB benchmark observations with labeled leak conditions and 22 pressure features. These are samples, not a count of independent physical leak events.

ΔP = P(baseline) − P(leak)

A residual describes how pressure changes relative to a baseline. Combining residuals across 22 locations creates a spatial signature of hydraulic imbalance.

Dataset and feature definition: thesis presentation, slides 10–11.

What I built

Feature and model pipeline

Prepared pressure residual features and implemented Random Forest and baseline classifiers using Python, pandas, NumPy, and scikit-learn.

Controlled simulation pipeline

Built an EPANET-based data-generation pipeline and designed a two-zone simulation architecture to support further validation.

Findings and their limits.

The presentation reports Random Forest as the strongest of the compared models in accuracy and stability. Its feature-importance analysis indicates that multiple pressure sensors contribute to predictions, consistent with a spatial pressure signal.

This is evidence from synthetic benchmark data, not demonstrated performance on a deployed residential system. The presentation does not specify scenario-level train/test separation, so it does not establish performance on entirely unseen leak scenarios.

A headline accuracy score is not included because the final evaluation output is unavailable for verification.

Findings: slides 13–14. Scope and future validation: slides 15–19.

What comes next

Validate a physical hardware testbed, introduce realistic sensor noise, explore temporal models such as CNNs and LSTMs, and build continuous monitoring interfaces. Acoustic and ultrasonic sensing are proposed extensions; the reported study evaluates pressure features.

Source: AI-Enabled Leak Detection in Simulated Urban Water Distribution Networks and Prospects for Residential-Level Usage, Mansi Aher, Spring 2026 thesis presentation. Advisor: Dr. Navid Amini.
